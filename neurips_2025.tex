\begin{abstract}
Attention sinks (AS) and massive activations (MA) are recurring extreme-token phenomena in decoder-only Transformers. Existing explanations are mostly forward-facing: they describe how spike-like activations, normalization, or attention geometry can co-produce sink behavior. We ask the complementary question: \emph{why are extreme token activations learned at all in pre-norm models, where magnitude is largely normalized away in the forward map?} Our answer is a backward one. We show that under causal attention, an attention sink is also a \emph{gradient sink} (GS): the sink token's value pathway aggregates downstream gradients with strength controlled by attention-column statistics. We then show that RMSNorm compresses backpropagated gradients roughly inversely with input RMS, making large token activations an effective response to localized gradient pressure. This yields a simple mechanistic chain: AS $\rightarrow$ GS $\rightarrow$ MA. Empirically, on small Llama-like models, gradient concentration is strongest at token~0 and is concentrated in the attention block; RMSNorm compression tracks activation scale almost inversely; and direct backward-path interventions show that the value path is the dominant carrier of this effect. Finally, we introduce \emph{V-scale}, a value-path gradient valve that leaves attention weights unchanged but attenuates value-side backpropagation. V-scale preserves or strengthens AS while substantially reducing MA, supporting GS as the more appropriate mediator between them.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Attention sinks---tokens that attract substantial attention mass despite carrying little semantic content---have become central to both practical and theoretical work on long-context inference, compression, and quantization \citep{xiao2023streamingllm, bondarenko2023quantizable}. Massive activations---token-wise activation outliers concentrated on a few special tokens---have likewise attracted attention because they are visually striking, mechanistically important, and harmful for low-bit deployment \citep{sun2024massive}. These two phenomena often co-occur on early tokens, especially token~0, and prior work has therefore often treated them from a forward view: massive activations help create sink-friendly representations, or both phenomena arise from a shared architectural effect \citep{sun2024massive, guo2024activedormant, sun2026spike}.

This paper studies a different question. In a pre-norm Transformer, both attention and MLP blocks operate on normalized inputs. Why, then, should optimization learn such extreme token magnitudes in the first place? A purely forward explanation is incomplete because the model is not just a function being executed; it is a function being \emph{trained}. Our starting point is therefore backpropagation rather than forward geometry.

We introduce \emph{Gradient Sink} (GS): concentration of token-wise backpropagated signal on sink tokens. The key observation is simple but consequential. In causal attention, the same token that receives attention mass from many later positions also receives value-path credit assignment from those positions. This induces unavoidable gradient aggregation on sink tokens. In a pre-norm architecture, RMSNorm attenuates gradients roughly as $1/\rms(x)$, so increasing the sink token's activation magnitude becomes an effective way to reduce that local gradient pressure. In this view, MA is not the direct mechanistic cause of AS; it is a learned compensation for GS.

Our claims are intentionally narrow. We do not claim to be the first to separate sinks from spikes or the first to modify attention so as to reduce both. In fact, recent work already shows architectural decoupling by changing normalization, gating, or softmax variants \citep{zuhri2025softpick, qiu2025gated, sun2026spike}. Our contribution is instead to identify the \emph{training-time mediator}. The central mechanistic chain is
\[
AS \;\Longrightarrow\; GS \;\Longrightarrow\; MA,
\]
with RMSNorm providing the compression channel and V-scale providing an alternative gradient valve.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item We formulate GS as the missing backward mediator between AS and MA, repositioning MA as a compensation to localized gradient pressure rather than as the direct cause of sink formation.
    \item We derive exact V/K/Q gradient identities for causal attention and make AS $\Rightarrow$ GS on the value path the central theorem, with dependence on attention-column statistics.
    \item We connect this to pre-norm optimization by showing that RMSNorm compresses backpropagated gradients approximately inversely with activation scale.
    \item We validate the mechanism empirically with gradient measurements, detach-style backward interventions, and V-scale, which suppresses MA while preserving strong AS.
\end{itemize}

\section{Preliminaries}
This section formalizes notations and introduces the observables used throughout the paper.
The model architecture below is standard and is included only to make the subsequent analysis precise.
Our methodological contribution in this section is the introduction of \emph{Bloat/Compress/Change} ratios of gradients, which are designed to expose the training-time mediator between attention sinks and massive activations.

\subsection{Pre-Norm Transformers}
We study decoder-only Llama-like Transformers with pre-norm residual blocks, RMSNorm, RoPE positional encoding, and SwiGLU MLPs \textcolor{red}{Ref of RMS Norm}\citep{touvron2023llama,su2024rope,shazeer2020gluvariants}. Let $\bm H^\ell = (\bm h_1^\ell,\dots,\bm h_T^\ell)^T \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times d_{\mathrm{model}}}$ denote the hidden states of $T$ tokens at layer $\ell$. Then the update of the Transformer layer can be written by
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \bm H^{\ell+\frac{1}{2}} &= \bm H^{\ell} + \mathrm{Attention}\left(\mathrm{RMSNorm}\left(\bm H^{\ell}\right)\right), \\
        \bm H^{\ell+1} &= \bm H^{\ell+\frac{1}{2}} + \mathrm{MLP}\left(\mathrm{RMSNorm}\left(\bm H^{\ell+\frac{1}{2}}\right)\right).
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
As we mentioned above, the MLP block is defaultly implemented by SwiGLU, which is a variant of GLU \citep{shazeer2017glu} and is widely used in recent LLMs \citep{touvron2023llama, zhao2024gemini}. RMSNorm is a simpler alternative to LayerNorm that has been widely adopted in pre-norm Transformers \citep{zhang2019rmsnorm, xiong2020layernorm}. The word ``pre-norm'' means that the normalization is applied before the residual addition, which is the opposite of the original post-norm design \citep{vaswani2017attention}. RMSNorm is applied token-wise, so the normalization of each token depends only on its own activation $\bm h_i$:
\[
    \mathrm{RMSNorm}(\bm h_i) = \bm\gamma \odot \frac{\bm h_i}{\rms(\bm h_i)},\quad \rms(\bm x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt d} \|\bm x\|^2, \quad \text{where } \bm\gamma \in \mathbb{R}^d \text{ is a learnable vector}.
\] 

The attention block can be either standard multi-head attention (MHA) \citep{vaswani2017attention} or a variant such as grouped-query attention (GQA) \citep{touvron2023llama}. For simplicity, we focus on a single attention head $h$ in the analysis.
With token position $t \in \{1,\dots,T\}$ and head dimension $d$, we have the standard attention QKV computation
\begin{equation}
    \bm q_t^h = R_t W_Q^h \bm h_t^h,\quad \bm k_t^h = R_t W_K^h \bm h_t^h,\quad \bm v_t^h = W_V^h \bm h_t^h,
\end{equation}
where $R_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ is the RoPE rotation matrix. Then under causal mask $M_{\mathrm{causal}}$, the attention matrix $A^h=[a_{tj}^h]$ and the attention output $\bm o_t^h$ are given by
\begin{equation}
    a_{tj}^h = \softmax\!\left(\frac{\ip{\bm q_t^h}{\bm k_j^h}}{\sqrt d} + M_{\mathrm{causal}}\right)_j,
    \quad \bm o_t^h = W_O^h \sum_{j\le t} a_{tj}^hv_j^h.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Metrics for Attention Sinks and Massive Activations}
We next define the forward observables to track attention sinks (AS) and massive activations (MA). Different prior papers used slightly different diagnostics \citep{sun2024massive,guo2024activedormant,sun2026spike} and here we want to choose continuous statistics that are convenient for theory and are stable across training.

\paragraph{Attention sink.}
For a candidate sink position $s$ (typically the first token), thresholded criteria are often used to count sink-like behavior and the Sink Rate is often defined as
\begin{equation}
    \text{Sink}_s^{\epsilon} = \frac{1}{LH} \sum_{l=1}^L\sum_{h=1}^H \mathbb{I}(\alpha_s^{l,h} > \epsilon),
    \quad
    \alpha_s^{l,h}=\frac{1}{T_\epsilon}\sum_{t=s}^{s+T_\epsilon-1} a_{ts}^{l,h},
\end{equation}
where $\epsilon$ is a predefined threshold. $L$ and $H$ are number of Transformer layers and number of heads in each layer, respectively. Following prior work we set $T_\epsilon=64$ \textcolor{red}{TODO}. In this paper, we also use the following simpler and continuous column statistics:
\begin{equation}
    M_s^{l,h} = \sum_{t=s}^T a_{ts}^{l,h},
    \quad
    S_s^{l,h} = \sum_{t=s}^T \left(a_{ts}^{l,h}\right)^2.
\end{equation}
The quantity $M_s^{l,h}$ measures how much total attention mass is routed through token $s$, while $S_s^{l,h}$ emphasizes stronger concentration and will appear naturally in our gradient analysis.

Layer-level summaries such as the sink-token curve $A_s^\ell$, the layer maximum $\max_t A_t^\ell$, or ratios against the mean over non-sink tokens are all derived from this base quantity.
We intentionally use a continuous norm rather than a hard outlier threshold, since our focus is on training dynamics rather than binary token classification.

\subsection{Gradient Ratios}
The central claim of this paper concerns the \emph{backward} mechanism.
This is the point where our notation departs from standard forward diagnostics.

\paragraph{Token-wise gradient magnitudes.}
For any intermediate tensor $z=\{z_t\}_{t=1}^T$, define the token-wise gradient magnitude
\begin{equation}
    G_t(z) \;=\; \norm{\nabla_{z_t}\mathcal L}_2 .
\end{equation}
In particular, on the attention path we will use
\begin{equation}
    G_t^{(Q)} = G_t(q), \qquad
    G_t^{(K)} = G_t(k), \qquad
    G_t^{(V)} = G_t(v).
\end{equation}
We say that position $s$ exhibits a \emph{Gradient Sink} on a given path if its token-wise gradient magnitude is disproportionately large relative to other positions.
Among these quantities, $G_s^{(V)}$ will play the most important role, since our main theorem shows that under causal attention, strong AS necessarily induces value-path gradient aggregation on sink tokens.

\paragraph{Local gradient-change factors.}
To track how gradient magnitude is reshaped inside a pre-norm residual branch, consider any local map
\begin{equation}
    f: u \mapsto w .
\end{equation}
We define its token-wise gradient-change factor by
\begin{equation}
    \Gamma_t(f)
    =
    \frac{\norm{\nabla_{u_t}\mathcal L}_2}
         {\norm{\nabla_{w_t}\mathcal L}_2}.
\end{equation}
Values larger than $1$ indicate gradient amplification through the local backward map, while values smaller than $1$ indicate attenuation.

We instantiate this definition on the two pre-norm branches.

\paragraph{Attention branch.}
For the map
\begin{equation}
    h_t^\ell
    \xrightarrow{\;\mathrm{RMSNorm}\;}
    \tilde h_t^\ell
    \xrightarrow{\;\mathrm{Attention}\;}
    r_t^{\mathrm{attn},\ell},
\end{equation}
we define
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{Bloat}_t^{\mathrm{attn},\ell}
    =
    \Gamma_t\!\big(\mathrm{Attention}: \tilde h_t^\ell \mapsto r_t^{\mathrm{attn},\ell}\big),
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{Compress}_t^{\mathrm{attn},\ell}
    =
    \Gamma_t\!\big(\mathrm{RMSNorm}: h_t^\ell \mapsto \tilde h_t^\ell\big),
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{Change}_t^{\mathrm{attn},\ell}
    =
    \Gamma_t\!\big(\mathrm{Attention} \circ \mathrm{RMSNorm}: h_t^\ell \mapsto r_t^{\mathrm{attn},\ell}\big).
\end{equation}
By construction,
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{Change}_t^{\mathrm{attn},\ell}
    =
    \mathrm{Bloat}_t^{\mathrm{attn},\ell}
    \cdot
    \mathrm{Compress}_t^{\mathrm{attn},\ell}.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{MLP branch.}
Analogously, for
\begin{equation}
    h_t^{\ell+\frac12}
    \xrightarrow{\;\mathrm{RMSNorm}\;}
    \tilde h_t^{\ell+\frac12}
    \xrightarrow{\;\mathrm{SwiGLU}\;}
    r_t^{\mathrm{mlp},\ell},
\end{equation}
we define
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{Bloat}_t^{\mathrm{mlp},\ell}, \qquad
    \mathrm{Compress}_t^{\mathrm{mlp},\ell}, \qquad
    \mathrm{Change}_t^{\mathrm{mlp},\ell}
\end{equation}
in the same way.

These ratios are introduced in this paper.
They are not meant as new phenomena in themselves; rather, they are measurement tools for answering the question left open by forward-only accounts:
\emph{where does gradient pressure accumulate during training, and how is that pressure subsequently reshaped by pre-norm computation?}

In the sections that follow, the key mechanism can be summarized as follows:
attention sinks concentrate backward signal on sink-token value states (large $G_s^{(V)}$ and large $\mathrm{Bloat}_s^{\mathrm{attn}}$), while RMSNorm provides an activation-dependent compression channel (small $\mathrm{Compress}_s$ when the activation norm is large).
This is the basis for our AS $\rightarrow$ GS $\rightarrow$ MA account.

\subsection{Theory: why an attention sink is also a gradient sink}
We only state the main results here; proofs are omitted in this short arXiv version and will appear in the full technical version.

\subsection{Exact causal-attention identities}
Let $g_t := \nabla_{y_t}\mathcal{L} = W_O^\top \nabla_{o_t}\mathcal{L}$ denote the upstream gradient at the value aggregation site.

\begin{theorem}[Exact value-path aggregation]
For any token index $s$,
\[
\nabla_{v_s}\mathcal{L} = \sum_{t=1}^T a_{ts} g_t.
\]
Therefore, the gradient on $v_s$ is an attention-weighted sum of all downstream gradients whose outputs read from token $s$.
\end{theorem}

This identity already exposes the mechanism. A sink token is not only a forward collector of attention mass; it is also a backward collector of credit assignment. If many later positions attend to token $s$, then many later gradients also pass through $v_s$.

A convenient quantitative version follows from a standard mean-plus-noise decomposition $g_t = \mu + \varepsilon_t$ for stochastic gradients \citep{mandt2017sgd, mccandlish2018noise}.

\begin{theorem}[Second-moment control by sink statistics]
Assume $g_t = \mu + \varepsilon_t$, where the residuals have bounded covariance. Then
\[
\mathbb{E}\big[\norm{\nabla_{v_s}\mathcal{L}}^2\big]
= M_s^2\norm{\mu}^2 + \text{noise terms},
\]
and in particular
\[
M_s^2\norm{\mu}^2 \le
\mathbb{E}\big[\norm{\nabla_{v_s}\mathcal{L}}^2\big]
\le M_s^2\norm{\mu}^2 + \sigma^2 S_s + \rho(M_s^2-S_s).
\]
Hence stronger sink columns imply systematically larger value-path gradient concentration.
\end{theorem}

The theorem makes AS $\Rightarrow$ GS on the value path the paper's central inevitability statement. It also clarifies why value-side interventions should matter most.

\paragraph{K-side and Q-side asymmetry.}
The same derivation yields
\[
\nabla_{k_s}\mathcal{L}=\sum_{t=1}^T a_{ts}\beta_{ts}\frac{q_t}{\sqrt d},
\qquad
\nabla_{q_s}\mathcal{L}=\sum_{j\le s} a_{sj}\beta_{sj}\frac{k_j}{\sqrt d},
\]
where $\beta_{ts}=\ip{g_t}{v_s-y_t}$. The crucial asymmetry is that K-side gradients aggregate \emph{column-wise}, while Q-side gradients are \emph{row-local}. Under causal masking, the first token attends only to itself, which gives $\nabla_{q_1}\mathcal{L}=0$ exactly. This matches our measurements: token~0 shows strong value/key gradient concentration but no special Q-side advantage.

\subsection{RMSNorm makes large activations a gradient compressor}
For RMSNorm \citep{zhang2019rmsnorm},
\[
\mathrm{RMSNorm}(x)=\gamma \odot \frac{x}{\rms(x)},\qquad \rms(x)=\sqrt{\frac{1}{d}\sum_i x_i^2}.
\]
Its Jacobian obeys a simple operator-norm bound.

\begin{proposition}[RMSNorm compression]
Let $J_{\mathrm{rms}}(x)$ be the Jacobian of RMSNorm. Then
\[
\norm{J_{\mathrm{rms}}(x)}_{\mathrm{op}} \le \frac{2\norm{\gamma}_\infty}{\rms(x)}.
\]
Consequently,
\[
\norm{\nabla_x\mathcal{L}} \lesssim \frac{1}{\rms(x)}\norm{\nabla_y\mathcal{L}}.
\]
Increasing the input norm reduces the backpropagated gradient norm.
\end{proposition}

This is the bridge from GS to MA. In a pre-norm Transformer, large token activation is not primarily useful because it changes the \emph{forward} map through magnitude; it is useful because it changes the \emph{backward} map through normalization. This is consistent with why pre-norm is favored for stable optimization \citep{xiong2020layernorm}.

\section{Empirical evidence for \texorpdfstring{$AS \rightarrow GS \rightarrow MA$}{AS -> GS -> MA}}

The familiar forward phenomena are reproduced: token~0 develops strong AS in mid layers; MA peaks are created by the previous-layer MLP; and sink tokens exhibit smaller value norms (VSD), consistent with prior observations \citep{sun2024massive, guo2024activedormant}. We then measure the backward side directly.

\section{Backward interventions identify the value path as the main carrier}
Forward interventions are often hard to interpret: changing a trained activation changes both direction and magnitude and can easily move the model out of distribution. We therefore intervene on the backward path while leaving the forward computation unchanged. We study five settings during training: baseline, \texttt{detach\_k0}, \texttt{detach\_v0}, \texttt{detach\_k0v0}, and \texttt{detach\_mlp\_norm\_out}. Across these runs, the training losses remain close (roughly 3.40--3.42 in our 0.1B experiments), but the extreme-token phenomena reorganize substantially.

The intervention pattern is decisive. \texttt{detach\_k0} only weakens the amplitude of AS/MA. In contrast, \texttt{detach\_v0} changes both the magnitude and the shape of the curves; token~0 loses its unique status and begins to resemble other early tokens. Joint detachment of K and V affects the phenomenon most strongly and can relocate sink behavior to other early positions. These results line up with the theory: the value path is the dominant carrier of the gradient-sink effect.

We stress, however, that detach-style experiments are still not clean causal evidence by themselves. They are useful for identifying the relevant path, but they hard-cut gradients. The decisive prediction of our mechanism is stronger: if we reduce gradient pressure \emph{without substantially changing attention weights}, then MA should fall while AS remains. That is the role of V-scale.

\section{V-scale: a value-path gradient valve}
We apply a simple radial transform to the value state,
\[
\hat v = \phi(r) v,\qquad r=\norm{v}^2,\qquad \phi(r)=\frac{r}{r+C},\quad C>0.
\]
Because attention weights depend only on $Q$ and $K$, this modification leaves the attention matrix itself unchanged. What it changes is the backward Jacobian through the value pathway.

\begin{proposition}[V-scale gradient valve]
The Jacobian of V-scale is
\[
J_\phi(v)=\phi(r)I + \frac{2C}{(r+C)^2} vv^\top.
\]
Its largest eigenvalue is
\[
\lambda_{\parallel}(r)=\frac{r^2+3Cr}{(r+C)^2} \le \frac{9}{8},
\]
and when $r \ll C$,
\[
\lambda_{\parallel}(r) \approx \frac{3r}{C} \ll 1.
\]
Thus low-norm value states receive strong gradient attenuation.
\end{proposition}

This is exactly the regime we want: sink tokens are often value-drained, so the extra valve is strongest where GS is strongest. Unlike architectural changes that directly alter softmax geometry \citep{bondarenko2023quantizable, zuhri2025softpick, qiu2025gated}, V-scale targets the backward channel more selectively.

The reduction is not explained by ``values simply becoming smaller''; the cleaner interpretation is that the value path now has an alternative gradient valve, so the model no longer needs as much MLP-created activation to stabilize backpropagation.

\section{Related Work}
Our framing is complementary to recent forward-view explanations rather than a contradiction of them. \citet{sun2024massive} showed that massive activations act as effective bias terms and can concentrate attention on specific tokens. \citet{guo2024activedormant} connected attention sinks, value-state drains, and residual peaks through an active-dormant mechanism. Very recent work argues that spikes and sinks can co-emerge from architectural choices such as pre-norm, sparse near-constant representations, and Q/K geometry, and can be separated by normalization or gating changes \citep{sun2026spike}. Other attention variants reduce or remove outliers by changing the forward normalization rule or adding explicit gates \citep{bondarenko2023quantizable, zuhri2025softpick, qiu2025gated}. Concurrently, \citet{qiu2026unified} argue that attention and residual outliers serve a broader rescaling role in training.

The difference is the object of explanation. Existing work mostly explains \emph{how} sink-friendly forward representations can arise or \emph{how} to alter the architecture to suppress them. Our paper asks \emph{why extreme token activations are learned at all} in a pre-norm model and answers with a backward dynamical mechanism. The distinctive prediction is therefore not merely that AS and MA can be decoupled, but that reducing gradient pressure while largely preserving attention structure should reduce MA. V-scale is designed precisely to test that prediction.

\section{Conclusion}
This paper argues that attention sinks should be viewed not only as a forward attention phenomenon but also as a backward optimization phenomenon. Under causal attention, sink tokens inevitably aggregate value-path gradients; in pre-norm Transformers, RMSNorm makes large token activations an effective way to compress that pressure. This yields a compact mechanistic account:
\[
\text{Attention Sink} \;\Rightarrow\; \text{Gradient Sink} \;\Rightarrow\; \text{Massive Activation}.
\]
Gradient measurements, backward-path interventions, and V-scale all support this picture. The result is not that MA is unimportant, nor that forward explanations are wrong. The result is that GS is the more appropriate mediator between AS and MA.

For an arXiv-first version, this already closes the minimal loop: a concrete mechanism, a theorem identifying the unavoidable aggregation path, measurements aligned with that theorem, and an intervention whose behavior matches the mechanism's decisive prediction. A fuller version should add complete proofs, larger-scale experiments, and downstream consequences for quantization and efficient inference.
